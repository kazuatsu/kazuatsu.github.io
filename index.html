Job Market Paper

The Keynesian Case for Trade Protection: A Modern Treatment

With Keelan Beirne

Paper forthcoming

We revisit the Keynesian rationale for tariffs: when downward nominal wage rigidity creates inefficient employment losses, tariffs can serve as second-best instruments by expanding labor demand in affected sectors. We characterize optimal tariffs in a dynamic multisector economy with wage rigidities and a flexible exchange rate. Our contribution is twofold. First, from an analytical standpoint, tariffs can be counterproductive instruments: they may reduce labor demand in the very sectors they are meant to protect, as currency appreciation depresses export demand and discourages current spending. As a result, optimal tariffs may be subsidies. Second, from a quantitative standpoint, optimal interventions and the associated welfare gains remain modest in our U.S. application even when labor distortions are sizable. Optimal tariffs are small because of their limited effectiveness in shifting employment across sectors relative to the distortions they create.

Task-Based Discrimination

With Erik Hurst and Yona Rubinstein

American Economic Review, 114(6): 1723–1768, 2024.

Published article · Working paper

We develop a task-based model of occupational sorting to identify and quantify the effect of discrimination, racial skill gaps, and aggregate task prices on Black-White differences in labor market outcomes over time. At the heart of our framework is the idea that the size and nature of racial barriers faced by Black workers vary by the task requirements of each job. We define a new task that measures the extent to which individuals interact with others as part of their job. We show that this measure is a good proxy for the extent of discrimination in the economy.

Romer or Ricardo?

With Chang-Tai Hsieh and Peter Klenow

Working paper, 2022; revision in progress.

Working paper

How do new varieties and quality improvements shape trade, growth, and the gains from foreign innovation? We develop a multicountry model combining new variety creation and international technology diffusion. On the theoretical front, we show that foreign growth through new variety creation raises home welfare by creating new sources of comparative advantage, while foreign growth through creative destruction can lower home welfare by eroding home’s comparative advantage and worsening its terms of trade. On the empirical front, we use product-level export and import dynamics to infer countries’ innovation patterns. The estimates suggest that the United States specializes in creating new varieties, while China relies more heavily on improving products developed abroad.
